Order execution costs

Empirical bps cost by category and order strategy—measured on live Interactive Brokers fills, not lifted from fee tables.

Most cost calculators copy fee schedules. We measure the actual fills: every column below comes from running real orders against an Interactive Brokers account at small notional and recording the slippage to mid, the time to fill, and the all-in commission.

The result: a category × strategy matrix that, paired with our recommended execution policy, tells you which order type to use for what. Limit at mid for wide spreads, the IB midprice algo for tight ones, plain market for FX (and as a fallback when the chain doesn't fill). The numbers are bps of notional, signed (positive = cost, negative = price improvement).

We re-run the harness on a regular cadence and update the matrix. Live data is the honest baseline; paper-account data fills cells where live samples are still thin. The page shows the best-available number per cell.

The four order types

Each strategy trades off price against certainty of execution differently. The matrix above measures all four on every category; our recommended policy picks one per spread band (see below).

Limit at mid. A limit order priced at the calculated midpoint of the bid/ask at submit. If it doesn't fill, the order is cancelled and re-submitted—up to four attempts on a 60-second budget. Best for wide spreads where waiting beats paying the spread; can fail to fill in fast-moving markets if the mid runs away from the price.

IB midprice algo. Interactive Brokers' hosted MIDPRICE algorithm. The algo waits internally (up to ~30 seconds) and fills at the prevailing midpoint at the moment of execution. Less control than the manual limit, but lower workload. Available on US equities and equity ETFs; not supported on futures, cash FX, or CFDs.

IB adaptive algo. IB's Adaptive algorithm at Normal priority. Sends a marketable limit that adjusts toward the touch as needed—smarter routing than plain market with active venue selection. Available on most asset classes but not on cash FX or CFDs.

Plain market. A standard market order. Fills immediately at the touch—no price guarantee but no waiting either. Universal—works on any instrument we trade.

Our recommended execution policy

Picking the cheapest cell from the matrix per instrument isn't robust—per-cell sample sizes are thin and the cheapest-measured strategy can flip with one more sweep. The honest move is to classify each order by its spread band at submit and follow a chain. Below is the policy we recommend.

Tight spread (≤ 3 bps). IB midprice algo first. If it doesn't fill within 35 seconds, drop to IB adaptive; if that doesn't fill, plain market. When the spread is small, retry overhead isn't worth it—let the algo find liquidity quickly and accept near-mid execution.

Grey zone (3-10 bps). Same chain as tight. The right strategy in this band is sensitive to the exact spread and remains a calibration question as more data accumulates.

FX—plain market by design. EURUSD spreads run around 0.17 bps. At that level the difference between picking the optimal strategy and just hitting the touch is in the noise—the delta is negligible. The midprice and adaptive algos aren't supported on cash FX anyway, so the realistic choice is limit-at-mid or plain market. Plain market wins on simplicity, and the cost difference is immaterial.

How we measure

The numbers above come from running real orders, not from broker fee tables. We submit small orders against an Interactive Brokers account across four strategies—limit-at-mid, IB midprice algo, IB adaptive algo, plain market—and record three things per fill: the slippage versus mid-price at submit time, the time to fill, and the all-in commission.

Slippage is signed: positive means you paid worse than mid (cost), negative means you got price improvement. Sample size is shown in each cell so you can judge how thin or fat the data is. Empty cells mean either the strategy isn't supported on that contract type, or live quote data wasn't available at submit time.

Where we have enough live-account data we use it. Otherwise we show paper-account data, which is reliable for spread, commission, and fill timing but slightly distorts limit-strategy slippage (paper fills limits at the mid deterministically). The matrix shows the raw measurement, including negative values when our sample captured price improvement. The calculator caps slippage at zero in its total so a forward-looking estimate never promises you'll be paid to trade. The full methodology and the exact harness code are open-source.

FAQ

Why measure trading costs instead of using broker fee tables?

Fee tables only show what the broker charges. They miss the two costs that actually dominate: the bid-ask spread (what market-makers earn, and the largest line on every trade outside illiquid names) and the strategy effect (a limit at mid can fill at near-zero cost while a market order pays the full half-spread on the same instrument). Measuring real fills captures both, on the same contracts under the same market conditions, so you can compare order types apples-to-apples.

Why do limit orders sometimes show negative cost (price improvement)?

When IB routes a marketable limit to a venue that fills inside the visible bid-ask — IEX D-Peg, midpoint matches, dark pools — the print comes back at a price better than the NBBO. The negative bps in the matrix is the size of that improvement. Paper accounts don't simulate this, so it only shows up on live fills. The matrix reports the raw measurement; the calculator caps slippage at zero so a forward-looking estimate never promises you'll be paid to trade.

What's the difference between the IB midprice algo and limit at mid?

Both target the mid, but they're managed differently. The IB midprice algo is a server-side algorithm hosted by Interactive Brokers — it waits up to ~30 seconds for liquidity at the prevailing midpoint and routes opportunistically to dark and midpoint venues. Limit at mid is a plain limit order priced at the midpoint at submit; if it doesn't fill we cancel and re-submit, up to four attempts on a 60-second budget. The midprice algo is hands-off and can capture sub-NBBO routing; limit at mid is more transparent and works on contracts where the algo isn't supported (futures, FX, CFDs).

Are these numbers good for large-size trades?

No. The harness uses tiny notional — one share, one contract, smallest tradable lot — so book depth, queue position, and market impact don't show up in the measurement. A small market order clearing the touch quote behaves very differently from one walking through three price levels. Treat the matrix as a floor on what tight execution can achieve, not a ceiling on what you'll pay at size.

Which broker did you measure?

Interactive Brokers Pro with tiered commission. Other brokers differ on commission schedule and on which algorithms they expose. The spread numbers should generalize — they reflect the public market, not IB's pricing — but commission, regulatory passthrough, and exact slippage will shift. The harness is open source under MIT; run it against your own broker to recalibrate.

How often does this update?

Whenever we run a new sweep — manual cadence, typically every few weeks during US market hours. The matrix snapshot you're looking at is rendered directly from the data in the public repo, so the page refreshes automatically when we push new results. If you want fresher numbers on your schedule, clone the repo and run the harness against your own paper or live account; one full sweep takes about ten minutes.
